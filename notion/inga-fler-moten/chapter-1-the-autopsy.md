# Chapter 1 — The Autopsy

It is Tuesday afternoon, and nobody in this room wants to be here.

You can tell because of the laptops. Half the people around the table have them open at angles that suggest they are listening while actually replying to emails about another meeting. The other half don't have laptops, which means they have their phones in their laps, thumbs moving. At the front of the room, someone is presenting slides about the marketing plan for the next quarter. The slides are, by objective measure, good slides. Clean fonts. Appropriate use of white space. A graph that clearly shows the thing going up.

Nobody is looking at the graph.

This is not unusual behavior. This is not a uniquely disengaged team or a uniquely boring presentation. This is every meeting in every organization in every sector of the professional economy, replicated with minor variations across every time zone that has ever hosted a conference call. The details change. The underlying choreography does not.

Here is the choreography: someone presents something. Everyone half-listens. Questions are asked, most of which could have been comments in a document. A decision is either deferred ("let's circle back on this"), made without being acknowledged as a decision, or overridden thirty minutes later by someone who wasn't in the room. The meeting ends. People stretch. Someone says "great chat." Nobody is quite sure what just happened or whether anything will be different as a result.

Then, because the calendar is a self-replicating organism, the next meeting starts.

— — —

This is a book about that meeting. More specifically, it is a book about what that meeting actually is — what's inside it when you cut it open — and about what the organizations and individuals who've gotten rid of it do instead.

Let's start with the cutting.

### What a Meeting Actually Costs

Before we perform the autopsy, we need to know the body count.

The numbers on meeting waste have been circulating for long enough that they've stopped shocking anyone, which is itself a form of institutional pathology. Atlassian found that employees attend an average of 62 meetings per month and consider more than half of them a waste of time. Microsoft's Work Trend Index reported that time spent in meetings more than doubled between 2020 and 2022 and has not meaningfully decreased since. Steven Rogelberg — who holds the remarkable academic distinction of being a professor whose entire career is devoted to the study of meetings — has calculated that unnecessary meetings cost the US economy approximately 37 billion dollars per year.

Thirty-seven billion dollars. Spent on rooms full of people looking at their phones.

But aggregate numbers have a numbing quality. Let's make it concrete. Take a single, unremarkable one-hour meeting — the kind that happens twenty, thirty, forty times a day across a mid-sized organization. Say there are ten people in the room. Say their average fully-loaded cost to the organization is the equivalent of a thousand euros per day, or roughly 125 euros per hour.

That meeting costs 1,250 euros. For one hour. Of ten people half-listening to a presentation about Q3.

Now look at your calendar for last week. Count the meetings. Multiply.

This is before we account for what economists call opportunity cost — the value of what those ten people could have been doing instead of sitting in that room. Which is, in every case, the actual work they were hired to do.

### Four Meetings, Four Autopsies

The modern organizational meeting has evolved — if that is the right word for a process that mostly involves getting worse — into four recognizable variants. Each has a stated purpose, a revealed purpose, and an actual outcome. These are rarely the same thing.

## I. The Status Update

The status update is the most common meeting in the organizational ecosystem, which makes it simultaneously the most expensive and the most unnecessary.

The premise: a group of people who are collectively responsible for a project or function assemble at regular intervals to report on the current state of their respective domains. Person A covers marketing. Person B covers engineering. Person C covers the thing that three months ago was called "strategic initiatives" and has since been rebranded "growth." Everyone listens politely. The status has been updated.

The logical question — which almost nobody asks, because it has the quality of pointing out that the emperor is naked and the emperor signs your performance review — is: why didn't they just read it?

Every piece of information conveyed in a status update meeting already exists somewhere. It is in a project management tool, a shared document, a spreadsheet, or the head of the person who is about to spend forty-five minutes narrating it at you. The meeting does not create the information. It transmits it, orally, at the pace of human speech, with all the attendant inefficiency that implies.

Reading is faster than listening. This is not an opinion. The average adult reads at roughly 250 words per minute and listens at roughly 150. Information conveyed in text can be skimmed, searched, returned to, shared, archived. Information conveyed in speech in a meeting can be misheard, interrupted, or forgotten before the meeting is over.

*The status update meeting is, at its core, a mechanism for producing the feeling of being informed in one person — at the expense of the productive time of many.*

There is a meaningful distinction between the feeling of being informed and the state of being informed. A manager who reads a shared project document has access to exactly the same information as one who just ran the status meeting. But only one of them feels like they know what's happening. The status update exists to serve that feeling. It is extraordinarily expensive feelings management.

## II. The Decision Meeting

The decision meeting is convened for a specific and ostensibly high-value purpose: to make a decision. A group of relevant stakeholders assembles, the options are laid out, the implications are discussed, a choice is made. Clean. Efficient. Participatory.

Here is what actually happens.

In the week before the meeting, the people who care most about the outcome have already had the real conversation. Over coffee, in a Slack thread, in a fifteen-minute call that was nominally about something else. By the time everyone is in the room, one of three things is true: the decision has already been made and the meeting will confirm it; the decision is genuinely contested and the meeting will surface the conflict without resolving it; or the decision requires information nobody has yet gathered, and the meeting will end with someone being tasked to gather it — which means another meeting.

The most honest description of the decision meeting is this: it is a venue for the formalization of conclusions reached elsewhere, occasionally interrupted by the discovery that they haven't been.

The tell is the phrase "let's take this offline." In meeting culture, "offline" is the zone where actual decisions happen. The meeting is "online" — visible, recorded, performative. The offline is where people say what they actually think, weigh what they actually care about, and make the choices they will subsequently present as having been made "in the meeting." When someone says "let's take this offline," they are acknowledging, without quite realizing it, that the meeting was the wrong venue for the conversation they're currently having.

## III. The Brainstorm

The brainstorm is the meeting type most likely to be described as "fun" and least likely to produce anything useful. It is also the one with the most comprehensively damning research record, which makes the fact that we keep scheduling it a minor miracle of institutional denial.

Alex Osborn invented brainstorming in 1953. He was an advertising executive, and the technique he developed — group ideation with explicit suspension of criticism — was premised on the belief that people think more creatively together than alone. Osborn was, by all accounts, a thoughtful and well-intentioned person. He was also wrong.

In 1958, researchers at Yale University tested Osborn's method against a control group of individuals working alone. The individuals working alone produced more ideas, and better ones. This result has been replicated so many times, across so many decades of organizational psychology, that it now constitutes one of the firmest findings in the field. Group brainstorming is inferior to individual ideation by almost every measurable metric: quantity of ideas, quality of ideas, originality of ideas.

The mechanisms are well understood. There is *production blocking*: only one person can speak at a time, so while one person talks, everyone else is either listening or composing — and doing neither well. There is *evaluation apprehension*: despite the stated rule that no ideas are bad ideas, people self-censor in groups in ways they don't when alone. There is *social loafing*: in a group, individuals reduce their cognitive effort in the implicit expectation that others will compensate. And there is a fourth effect that receives less attention but is in some ways the most damaging: once the first few ideas are on the table, everyone in the room generates variations on them. The group converges on a creative neighborhood and stays there. The stickies on the whiteboard are all, on closer inspection, the same sticky.

*The brainstorm does not produce better ideas. It produces faster convergence on mediocre ones — in a way that feels productive while systematically suppressing the difficult, solitary process by which actual creative insight is generated.*

## IV. The Alignment Meeting

The alignment meeting is the most dangerous of the four types, because it is the most nebulous. It has no clear deliverable, no measurable outcome, and no natural endpoint. It is also, in many organizations, the most common.

The premise: a group of people or teams need to "get aligned" — to share a common understanding of direction, priority, approach. Legitimate organizational need. Catastrophic vehicle for meeting it.

Alignment is not a feeling. It is not achieved by being in a room together, nodding at the same slide, and leaving with the shared impression that everyone is "on the same page." That feeling of shared understanding is extraordinarily fragile and almost certainly illusory. Research on communication consistently shows that people dramatically overestimate how well they have been understood. The speaker believes they have been clear; the listener has constructed their own interpretation, which may or may not resemble the speaker's intent; neither party is aware of the gap.

True alignment — the kind where different people, in different contexts, making different decisions, remain coherent with each other — is achieved not by a meeting but by a document. A document clear enough, specific enough, and accessible enough that anyone who needs to act on it can do so without further instruction. The alignment meeting is often a symptom of the absence of that document. It is what organizations do instead of writing things down.

The most telling sign of an alignment meeting is what happens immediately after it ends: someone schedules a follow-up. The follow-up is necessary because the original meeting produced a feeling of alignment without the substance of it. The follow-up will do the same. Eventually there will be a deck. The deck will require a meeting to present it.

### The Theater

Here is the observation that underlies all four of these autopsies, and that the rest of this book is concerned with explaining and addressing.

Most meetings are not about doing work. They are about *performing* work.

The difference is not subtle. Doing work produces something: a decision, a design, a paragraph, a line of code, a solved problem. Performing work produces the impression of having done work, which is a social and organizational artifact rather than an economic one.

The Q3 marketing presentation — twelve people, forty-five minutes, 1,250 euros — produced a performance of engagement with the marketing strategy. It did not produce the strategy, which already existed. It did not improve the strategy, because presentations are not designed for critique. It did not help anyone do their jobs better, because the information conveyed was either already known or available elsewhere. It produced, for the person who organized it, the feeling of having communicated something important. It produced, for most people in the room, the feeling of having participated in the organization. It produced, for a small number of people who were only marginally relevant to Q3 marketing, forty-five minutes of inbox catchup.

What it did not produce is something of value.

This is not a criticism of the people involved. The presenter was doing what presenters do. The attendees were doing what attendees do. Everyone was behaving rationally within the rules of a game they didn't design and have never been asked to question. The problem is not the players. The problem is the game.

And the question — the one this book is organized around — is where the game came from.

Because the status update, the decision meeting, the brainstorm, the alignment meeting: these are not forms that emerged spontaneously from the nature of knowledge work. They were imported from somewhere else. And when you trace them back to their origins, what you find is not modern organizational theory. What you find is a general giving orders to his officers. And a clerk counting cargo on a colonial dock.

That is where we are going next.

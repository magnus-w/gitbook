Reference: *Nobody Needs This Meeting* — non-fiction proposal | Referred by *Indra Sinha*

Indra Sinha read an essay I wrote on why the modern meeting is a relic of organizational cultures that no longer exist. He said: turn this into a book. This is that book.

*Nobody Needs This Meeting* is a work of narrative non-fiction — part intellectual history, part organizational diagnosis, part practical manual — that makes a single, unfashionable argument: the meeting is not broken. It is doing exactly what it was designed to do. The problem is that it was designed for a military command structure and a colonial counting house, not for the knowledge economy. We inherited it by accident. We can discard it by design.

The book tracks the meeting from its origins — the military briefing, the East India Company's administrative infrastructure, the industrial factory floor — through its migration into the modern office, where it arrived like the wrong organ transplanted into the wrong body and somehow kept beating. It then turns to the organizations that have figured out what to do instead: not how to run better meetings, but how to do the actual work of thinking, deciding and building together without a calendar invite in sight. Cases include product development teams in Silicon Valley, customer experience redesign at TUI, and structural change at HSB, Sweden's largest housing cooperative.

The argument rests on a claim Peter Drucker made thirty years ago and that most organizations have spent thirty years ignoring: knowledge workers know their own work better than anyone above them in the org chart. The meeting, structurally, is premised on the opposite assumption. That contradiction is the book.

I have spent two decades working at the intersection of product development, organizational design and customer experience across Silicon Valley, London, Berlin and Stockholm — as both an embedded leader and a consultant at organizations ranging from early-stage startups to TUI and HSB. I have lectured on this subject at the Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm and at MITX. The book draws on that experience, on original interviews with practitioners across sectors, and on the body of organizational research that has been quietly proving this thesis for years while everyone kept booking the conference room.

The book will be published simultaneously in English and Swedish. It is estimated at 60,000 words. It will sit alongside *Deep Work* and *It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work* on the shelf — and disagree productively with both.

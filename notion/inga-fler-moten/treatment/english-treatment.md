# English treatment

## NOBODY NEEDS THIS MEETING
### *How the knowledge economy inherited the wrong rituals — and what to do about it*

---

## THE HOOK

It's a Tuesday afternoon somewhere in Stockholm, and twelve people are sitting in a glass-walled conference room watching a PowerPoint presentation about the Q3 marketing plan. The slides are well-designed. The presenter is competent. The information is — and this is the part nobody says out loud — information that every single person in that room either already knows, could read in the shared drive in four minutes, or doesn't actually need at all.

This is not an unusual meeting. This is every meeting.

After forty-five minutes, the presentation ends. Someone asks a clarifying question that could have been a comment in a document. Someone else nods thoughtfully. The meeting organizer says "great, let's take the action items offline" — which is, when you think about it, an admission that nothing actionable just happened. Twelve people stand up, stretch, check their phones, and walk back to their desks. Twelve people who, combined, represent several hundred thousand euros of annual salary. Twelve people who, in the next thirty minutes before their next meeting, will attempt to do the work that the meeting was supposed to be *about*.

The scene is identical whether it's a glass tower in Stockholm, an open-plan loft in Berlin, a WeWork in London or a converted warehouse in San Francisco. The technology changes. The catering changes. The meeting does not change.

This book is about why — and about the organizations and individuals who found the exit.

---

## THE THESIS

The modern meeting was not designed for knowledge work — it was borrowed, almost accidentally, from two organizational cultures that are now extinct: the command-and-control military and the industrial administration office. This matters because knowledge workers are not assembly-line operators or colonial clerks; their expertise, judgment and creative agency are precisely what organizations are paying for — and meetings, structurally, are designed to bypass all three. The solution is not a better meeting. It is a different way of working entirely — one that replaces the ritual of being told things with the reality of building things together, and that assumes competence rather than endlessly managing around its absence.

---

## WHERE MEETINGS ACTUALLY CAME FROM

Here is a fact that should be more unsettling than it is: the two organizational cultures that gave us the modern meeting are both, by their own admission, done with it.

The first is the military. The command-and-control model — superior informs subordinates of orders, subordinates execute, superior monitors compliance and updates orders as needed — is the direct ancestor of the briefing, the debrief, the status meeting and the all-hands. It assumes a strict hierarchy of information, where the person at the top knows most and the people at the bottom know least. Modern militaries, with some irony, have almost entirely abandoned this model in favor of what they call *mission command*: leaders define the objective with maximum clarity, and the people closest to the action decide how to achieve it. The people who invented top-down information-as-meeting no longer practice it.

The second ancestor is more surprising. The modern administrative meeting — the status update, the progress report, the cross-departmental check-in — has its roots not in any office, but in the logistics infrastructure of colonial trade companies. The East India Company, managing the movement of cargo across three continents, invented the modern accounting department out of necessity: someone had to count what was on the boat, what was on the dock, what was in the warehouse, and reconcile those numbers across time zones and transport delays. They created systems of reporting that were, for their moment, genuinely sophisticated. Those systems — report up, aggregate, present to decision-makers — migrated into the factory, then into the corporation, and then into the knowledge economy, where they arrived like a fax machine at a cryptocurrency startup: technically functional, completely wrong for the environment.

The irony, again, is perfect. The factory floor — the industrial setting that made the status-update meeting feel natural — doesn't use it anymore either. Lean manufacturing, real-time production data, autonomous teams with embedded quality control: the factory figured out that the meeting-as-status-update was a bottleneck and removed it. The knowledge workers in the office did not get the memo.

## THE PRESENT REALITY

The cost of this institutional amnesia is not abstract.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index puts the average professional in back-to-back meetings for a substantial portion of their working week, with meeting time having roughly doubled since 2020. Atlassian's research finds that employees consider a significant majority of their meetings unnecessary. Steven Rogelberg at the University of North Carolina has calculated that unnecessary meetings cost the US economy alone somewhere in the range of $37 billion annually. These numbers are cited, reported and then, with beautiful irony, discussed in a meeting.

But the aggregate statistics miss something more specific and more damaging: what meetings do to the particular kind of worker who now constitutes the majority of the professional workforce in developed economies. Peter Drucker defined the knowledge worker in the 1990s with a precision that still holds: this is a person whose primary instrument of production is their own knowledge, and who therefore knows more about how to do their job than any manager above them in the hierarchy. The defining characteristic of knowledge work is that it cannot be supervised the way factory work can be supervised. You cannot stand behind a software architect and tell her how to think.

And yet the meeting — in almost all its contemporary forms — is structurally premised on exactly that assumption. The status update assumes the person running the meeting needs to know what the people in the meeting are doing. The presentation assumes that information flows more efficiently from one person speaking to a room than from a document shared with that room. The "alignment meeting" assumes that alignment is achieved by putting people in proximity rather than by producing a shared artifact they can react to. Every one of these assumptions is the Command & Control or the colonial counting house, wearing a Patagonia vest.

The organizations that have broken out of this are not all tech startups with ping-pong tables and no HR department. They span sectors, scales and geographies — and their experiences form the empirical backbone of this book.

At **IXI** in Silicon Valley, the decision to restructure entire product development cycles around written communication and asynchronous collaboration — rather than sprint planning meetings and daily standups — reduced time-in-meeting by a significant margin while measurably accelerating decision speed. At **TUI**, one of the world's largest travel companies, cross-functional teams working on customer experience redesign found that replacing weekly alignment meetings with a shared, living document that everyone could read, comment on and update in real time didn't just save time, it produced better thinking, because people had time to actually think before responding. At **HSB**, Sweden's largest housing cooperative, the introduction of agency-driven project teams with minimal meeting structure and maximum written transparency changed not just productivity metrics but retention: people stayed because they felt trusted to do their jobs.

The pattern across all three — and across the dozens of individuals interviewed for this book — is consistent and striking. The shift is never just about meetings. It is always about what you believe about the people doing the work.

## THE ALTERNATIVE: WHAT YOU DO INSTEAD

This is where most books on this subject fail. They diagnose with precision and prescribe with vagueness. "Be more intentional about your calendar." "Only hold meetings when absolutely necessary." These are not instructions. They are fortune cookies with a LinkedIn following.

The alternative to the meeting is not the absence of the meeting. It is a specific, learnable, replicable way of working that can be implemented at the level of an individual, a team, or an entire organization and that produces better outcomes by design, not by accident.

It has three core components:

**1. Writing as the primary instrument of collaboration.**
Not email. Not Slack messages. Not bullet-pointed meeting notes. *Actual writing,* the kind that requires you to form a complete thought before you share it. Organizations that have moved away from meetings as their primary coordination mechanism have, almost universally, become better writing organizations. Amazon's famous six-page memo. GitLab's handbook-first culture, where every decision, policy and process is written down before it is discussed. Basecamp's decades-long practice of written proposals, written decisions, written retrospectives. Writing forces clarity in a way that speaking in a room does not. It also creates a record, which means decisions stay made, and the meeting to re-discuss the already-decided thing becomes unnecessary by default.

**2. Asynchronous decision-making with explicit time boundaries.**
The most common justification for the meeting is that it is the fastest way to make a decision. This is almost never true, and can be empirically tested by anyone who has experienced the "let's take this offline" moment that ends a meeting that was called to make a decision. Asynchronous decision-making — share the proposal, give people 48 hours to respond, record the decision and move on — is faster for most decisions, more inclusive for all decisions, and produces better-documented outcomes than the meeting that nominally achieves the same result. It also has the significant advantage of not requiring twelve people to synchronize their calendars.

**3. Radical transparency as a substitute for status updates.**
The status update meeting exists because people don't know what's happening and want to find out. The solution is not to have fewer status update meetings. It is to make the status of everything visible to everyone, all the time, without a meeting being required to surface it. Modern project infrastructure — whether Notion, Jira, Slack, or well-maintained shared documents — already makes this technically possible for most organizations. The barrier is almost never technological. It is cultural: specifically, it is the manager who needs to *be told* things rather than knowing how to *go and read* them.

## CHAPTER BREAKDOWN

**Chapter 1 — The Autopsy**
What actually happens in a meeting. A forensic examination of the four most common meeting types — status update, decision meeting, brainstorm, alignment — and what each one costs versus what it produces. The numbers, and the theater.

**Chapter 2 — Where It Came From**
The military briefing, the colonial counting house, and the factory floor: a short history of how organizational cultures that no longer exist became the templates for how we work today. The archaeology.

**Chapter 3 — The Wrong Inheritance**
How the transition to knowledge work happened without a corresponding transition in organizational practice. Drucker's definition of the knowledge worker as the structural argument against the meeting. What changed in the work, and what didn't change in the office.

**Chapter 4 — The Cost of the Performance**
The aggregate data on meeting waste, and what the research says about meetings and creativity, decision quality, and psychological safety. Why the "empty theater" is not just annoying — it is actively destructive to the conditions that produce good knowledge work.

**Chapter 5 — Organizations That Found the Exit**
IXI, TUI, HSB, and others: how organizations across sectors and geographies restructured around agency rather than attendance. What they did, what they stopped doing, and what happened to their output when they did.

**Chapter 6 — Writing Is the Meeting**
The case for written communication as the primary instrument of collaboration. How to write for decision rather than discussion. What good async communication looks like and what distinguishes it from an email nobody reads.

**Chapter 7 — How Decisions Actually Get Made**
Asynchronous decision-making in practice: the formats, the timelines, the common failure modes, and how to build a decision culture that doesn't require a room. With specific examples from organizations that do this well.

**Chapter 8 — Transparent by Default**
Radical transparency as a status update killer. How to build an information environment where the answer to "what's the status of X?" is always one click away — and why the barrier to doing this is almost never technical.

**Chapter 9 — The Meetings You Actually Need**
There are meetings that are irreplaceable: genuine creative collision, emotionally complex conversations, the moments that require physical or synchronous presence. How to identify them, protect them, and make them count — precisely because they are no longer competing with forty hours of everything else.

**Chapter 10 — The Implementation**
A practical, sequenced guide to making the shift — for individuals working within resistant organizations, for team leads with the authority to experiment, and for organizational leaders ready to make it structural. Not a framework. A set of moves.

## AUTHOR

The author has spent two decades at the intersection of product development, organizational design and customer experience — across Silicon Valley startups, international corporations and European scale-ups. Having worked both as an embedded leader and an external consultant in environments ranging from early-stage tech companies in San Francisco to organizations of tens of thousands of people including TUI and HSB in Sweden, the perspective brought to this book is neither that of the academic observing from a distance nor the consultant selling a proprietary methodology.

It is the perspective of someone who has sat in the bad meeting, run the bad meeting, and — eventually — figured out how to get rid of it.

The approach to cross-functional team design, with a particular focus on knowledge worker agency and the conditions that produce high-output creative collaboration, has been the subject of lectures at the Royal Institute of Technology (KTH) in Stockholm and at MITX. The research underlying this book draws on that academic work as well as on original interviews with practitioners across sectors.

The book will be written in both English and Swedish, with the Swedish edition positioned for the Nordic business market — where the cultural emphasis on flat hierarchies and worker autonomy makes the argument not just timely but overdue.

## MARKET & COMPARABLE TITLES

**Primary market:** Knowledge workers, team leads, and organizational leaders in professional services, tech, media, consulting and the broader "office economy." Secondary market: HR and organizational development professionals.

**Comparable titles:**

- *Deep Work* — Cal Newport (the case for protected thinking time; this book is the organizational complement)
- *It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work* — Fried & Heinemeier Hansson (the practitioner manifesto; this book has more research and more cases)
- *Thinking in Systems* — Donella Meadows (for readers who want to understand *why* the system produces the bad outcome, not just that it does)
- *The Burnout Society* — Byung-Chul Han (the philosophical register, minus the accessibility; this book is what Han would write if he wanted people to actually change something)
- *Surrounded by Idiots* — Thomas Erikson (this book blames individual behavior for dysfunction at work, where we show that most of it comes from institutionalized malpractice that is easily changed)

**Why now:** The post-pandemic return-to-office debate has reanimated every question about *why* we work the way we do and produced almost no satisfying answers. AI is beginning to automate exactly the kind of low-agency, repetitive coordination work that most meetings are a symptom of. The ground is shifting and the cultural conversation is ready for a book that names what's actually happening.
